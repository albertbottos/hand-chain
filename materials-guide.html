Materials

Gold Filled vs Gold Plated vs Sterling Silver

Updated 24 November 2026

Three labels cover most hand chains sold in the US, and they describe different constructions rather than grades of the same thing. The metal decides how the piece ages and how much care it needs, so it is worth knowing what each label actually promises.

Sterling silver

Sterling silver is an alloy: 92.5 percent silver and 7.5 percent other metals, usually copper. Pure silver is too soft for a chain that crosses a moving hand; the alloy gives it enough strength to hold fine links without bending.

Sterling tarnishes. The copper in the alloy reacts with sulfur in the air, and the piece darkens over time, first in the recesses of the links. Tarnish is a surface layer, not damage, and it comes off with a silver polishing cloth. Store sterling in a closed pouch or a tarnish-resistant box and it needs polishing far less often.

Gold filled

Gold filled is not plating. A layer of gold is bonded to a base metal, usually brass or copper, under heat and pressure so the two become a single sheet before the chain is made. In the US, a piece can be labeled gold filled only when the gold makes up at least one twentieth of the item's total weight, which is where the "14/20" and "12/20" stamps come from: 14 karat or 12 karat gold at one twentieth by weight.

Because the gold layer is thick relative to plating and bonded rather than deposited, gold filled pieces keep their color through years of normal wear, though the hardest contact points can still wear through eventually.

Gold filled costs more than plated and much less than solid gold; for a hand chain worn often, it behaves closest to fine jewelry.

Gold plated

Gold plated pieces have a thin layer of gold applied by electroplating over a base metal. The plating is measured in microns, and it is thin enough that friction matters.

A hand chain is a demanding shape for plating. The chain rubs against the ring, the clasp opens and closes against itself, and the bracelet moves against the wrist. Those contact points are where plating wears first, and once the layer is gone the base metal shows through.

Plated pieces are the most affordable entry point and a reasonable choice for a trend piece or an occasion. They are the least suited to daily wear on a hand that types, writes and lifts all day.

Vermeil, briefly

Vermeil is sterling silver with a thick gold plating: in the US, at least 2.5 microns of at least 10 karat gold over sterling. It sits between gold filled and plated in durability, and the silver underneath is sterling rather than brass.

Solid gold

Solid gold hand chains are usually 14 karat, which is 58.3 percent gold, or 18 karat at 75 percent. The rest is alloy, added for strength and color. Solid gold cannot wear through because there is no layer to wear through, but it is the most expensive option by a wide margin, and 24 karat is too soft for a chain in this format.

How to choose

Match the metal to how often you will wear it: gold filled or sterling for several days a week, solid gold if the budget allows.

Read the label closely. "Gold tone", "gold finish" and "gold color" mean plated or coated, not gold filled. "14k" alone means solid 14 karat. "14/20" means gold filled.

Care by material

Sterling: polish with a silver cloth, store dry and closed, remove before swimming.

Gold filled: wipe with a soft dry cloth, remove before swimming and the gym, store separately so harder pieces do not scratch it.

Plated: keep it dry, put it on last after lotion and perfume, take it off before showering or sleeping, and do not polish it with an abrasive cloth.
