Types of Hand Chains: Styles Explained

Updated 24 November 2026

Every hand chain connects a ring to a wrist piece, but the way the chain is built changes how the piece looks and how it behaves on a moving hand. The categories below cover almost every design sold today, and each one has a different answer to the same question: how much should the chain do on the back of the hand?

Single-strand hand chains

One chain runs from a single ring to a clasped bracelet. This is the classic version and the easiest to live in. There is only one line across the hand, so it sits flat, rarely tangles and does not compete with a watch or a stack of thin bracelets.

Single-strand pieces suit a first hand chain and a daily-wear piece. They also take the least adjustment: if the bracelet fits, the chain drop is usually right, because there is no second strand to pull the first one out of line.

Multi-strand hand chains

Two or more chains run in parallel, or cross in a loose lattice, between the ring and the wrist. The bracelet is usually wider and heavier to carry the extra strands and to stop them splaying apart.

Multi-strand designs read as more decorative and more deliberate. They sit best on a hand kept free of other rings, since the strands need clear space across the knuckles. They are also the style most likely to need readjustment through the day, because each strand can shift independently.

Charm hand chains

A charm hand chain keeps a single chain but adds a small charm, coin or stone that rests on the back of the hand. The charm is the focal point, so the chain and ring are usually kept plain.

This style is a good compromise between a minimal chain and a more decorative piece. On a very small hand, an oversized charm can feel heavy and pull the chain to one side, so check the charm against the width of the back of your hand.

Cuff-linked hand chains

Instead of a clasped bracelet, these pieces attach to a rigid or semi-rigid cuff. The cuff holds the shape of the piece and keeps the chain in a fixed position, which makes the drape more predictable.

The trade-off is fit. A cuff has to match the wrist closely, and it is far less forgiving than a chain bracelet with an extender. Cuff-linked pieces are better for occasional wear and for anyone who likes a sculptural look at the wrist.

Multi-ring hand chains

A branching chain links two or three rings across adjacent fingers, sometimes with a midi ring on the upper phalanx. These pieces look striking and are the most demanding to wear: the rings fix the spacing of your fingers, so the chain pulls whenever you spread your hand.

Multi-ring designs suit photographs, events and anyone who enjoys a statement. For daily wear, they are best kept to two rings on neighbouring fingers rather than three across the whole hand.

Fixed versus adjustable construction

Beyond the chain pattern, hand chains split into fixed and adjustable builds. A fixed piece has a set bracelet length and a set chain drop; it fits if your measurements match. An adjustable piece uses a slide closure or an extender, so the wrist fit and the drape can both be tuned.

Adjustable is the safer choice for a first purchase and for buying as a gift, since it covers a range of wrist sizes.

Which type to choose

Choose single-strand for everyday wear and for a hand that already has a watch or rings on it. Choose multi-strand or charm if you want the back of the hand to be the focal point. Choose cuff-linked if you prefer a structured wrist and know your cuff size. Choose multi-ring if the piece is for occasions and you are comfortable with your fingers being linked.

Whichever style you pick, fit depends on three measurements rather than one, and the sizing guide explains what to measure. For stacking combinations, the layering guide covers which styles sit best with rings and a watch, and the comparison with a bracelet covers the basic format choice.
